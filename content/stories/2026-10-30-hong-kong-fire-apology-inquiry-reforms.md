![High-rise residential towers wrapped in green scaffolding netting at dusk, thick white smoke billowing in front of lit windows, several people seen from behind on a wet street lit by orange streetlamps.](/images/stories/hong-kong-fire-apology-inquiry-reforms.webp)

Hong Kong's chief executive, John Lee, apologised Thursday for government shortcomings that turned a small blaze into a fire that killed 168 people at a residential estate last year, the city's deadliest disaster in decades. Mr. Lee and seven other officials bowed to reporters as the government accepted and released the report of a judge-led independent committee, which pointed to "decades of failure" by the bodies that oversee building safety.

The fire began in November 2025 at Wang Fuk Court, a public housing complex in the Tai Po district then shrouded in bamboo scaffolding and netting for renovation work. A small fire, probably started by a cigarette, grew into an inferno that enveloped seven of the estate's eight towers. It was the world's deadliest residential fire since 1980.

The 1,154-page report, issued 11 months after the blaze, attributed the tragedy to faults and deliberate misconduct by private sector players and to long-running government neglect. It cited ignored warnings, substandard building materials and disabled fire alarms. The committee assigned the largest share of official responsibility to the Independent Checking Unit under the Housing Bureau, faulting it for a lack of oversight of the renovation work. The Fire Services Department and the Labour Department were also criticised. Had the departments held a "sense of crisis" about the risks and closed loopholes in the regulatory regimes, the report said, there was a "real possibility" the hazards could have been averted.

Mr. Lee announced a package of reforms on the same day. Housing secretary Winnie Ho resigned, the only official to step down so far. The apology did not settle the question of accountability: survivors and bereaved families have been waiting to see how the government addresses who is to blame for the ignored warnings, the substandard materials and the disabled alarms, and some said after Thursday's briefing that justice had yet to be done.

The report marks the fullest official accounting of the disaster since it occurred. Whether further consequences follow for officials or for the companies that carried out the renovation work remained unresolved at the close of the briefing.
