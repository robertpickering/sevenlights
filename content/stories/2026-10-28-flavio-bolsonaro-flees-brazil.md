Flávio Bolsonaro, a Brazilian senator and the eldest son of former president Jair Bolsonaro, has left Brazil days after losing Sunday's presidential runoff to President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva, according to Brazilian and Argentine reports. Allies told the Brazilian site Metrópoles that he would land in Florida on Tuesday afternoon and planned to request political asylum, adding that the Trump administration had been informed.

His departure came before an 11 a.m. Tuesday deadline to hand his passports to federal police. A Supreme Court justice had canceled the documents minutes after the runoff result and barred him from leaving the country as part of a criminal case in which police are investigating an alleged money-laundering operation. Bolsonaro denied wrongdoing in the probe. In a video posted Monday he said he had never thought about leaving the country. "I will hand over my passports and defend myself against this travesty, which is a clear attempt to intimidate me," he said. He did not comply.

The Argentine news agency Telam reported that Bolsonaro, his wife and two daughters entered Argentina and boarded a private flight bound for Guayaquil, Ecuador, operated by the Buenos Aires company Baires Fly. A senior Brazilian Federal Police official said he had not exited Brazil through any regular migration point. Reuters reported that one source close to the family said Bolsonaro had landed in Florida. A State Department source told the agency that it "will issue visas to Flavio Bolsonaro and his family and allow them entry into the United States."

The Supreme Court cited the risk of flight and Bolsonaro's "significant financial resources" in ordering the passports seized, according to a ruling seen by Reuters. The senator, who has held Italian citizenship since 2023 and reportedly possesses an Italian passport, loses his immunity from prosecution when his Senate term ends in February. Brazilian police are investigating claims about how he left the country.

Bolsonaro's candidacy had been cleared only in September and his defeat on Sunday capped a turbulent campaign. His departure appeared to catch his own aides by surprise, and President Lula's government has offered no public comment on the senator's whereabouts beyond noting that the investigations remain open.

Update, Oct. 29: Mr. Bolsonaro arrived at Miami-Opa Locka Executive Airport on Wednesday and, hours later, posted an open letter on X declaring himself "the elected president." He deleted that version and replaced it with a letter calling himself the rightful "leader" of Brazil. "I want to say, not only to my 60 million voters but to the Brazilian people, that I am the leader and that the criminals who stole Brazil's election are part of a criminal organisation that has established a tyranny," he wrote. He thanked Argentina's president, Javier Milei, "for his courage, sense of justice and legality," and President Trump "and the wonderful American people for welcoming me"; Mr. Trump granted him and his family entry visas. Asked about the letter at Joint Base Andrews, Mr. Trump declined to comment and reiterated his view that it was "a very strange election."

Mr. Bolsonaro grounded his rejection of the result on a two-hour voting extension granted by a Supreme Court justice appointed by Mr. Lula; fewer than 35,000 votes, some 0.03 per cent of the total, were cast in that period, according to an analysis by Insper professor Pedro Burgos. His own campaign had also sought an extension for rain-hit polls in conservative southern states. He lost by about 4.5 million votes. "I did not flee Brazil," he wrote. "I left Brazil, but I did not abandon the Brazilian people." Electoral authority chief Kassio Nunes Marques said the vote took place "in full compliance with legal and constitutional requirements," and Mr. Bolsonaro's own party recognised the result on Monday. President Lula called it "shameful" that the senator had not stayed in Brazil to prove his innocence.
