From Seven Lights, this is your Daily Brief for Saturday, October twenty-fourth, two thousand twenty-six.

Here are the headlines. President Vladimir Putin tells a special American envoy that Moscow is ready in principle to resume peace talks on Ukraine. France opens a terrorism investigation after a shooting near Paris kills one person and wounds five. Typhoon Chopai passes Taiwan with one death and more than five hundred injuries. Wall Street weighs the possibility of a merger between SpaceX and Tesla. And in the United States, courts and prosecutors move quickly in election-related cases, less than two weeks before voters go to the polls.

We begin with diplomacy. President Putin told Steven Witkoff, the special envoy of United States President Donald Trump, that Moscow is in principle ready to resume stalled, American-mediated peace talks with Ukraine. Kremlin foreign policy aide Yuri Ushakov said the two men met at the Constantine Palace in Saint Petersburg. He said negotiations could take place in November, with the main topic being what he called the building of security.

It was Witkoff's ninth visit to Russia for talks with Putin. The meeting came two days after he met Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky in the Norwegian capital, Oslo. Mr Witkoff and President Trump's son-in-law, Jared Kushner, have led the American negotiating effort since Mr Trump made ending the war a priority at the start of his term.

Kyiv and Moscow remain far apart. The Kremlin has offered no cease-fire and insists on full control of the Donetsk region, territory its forces have been unable to seize. President Trump has voiced doubts about Mr Zelensky's commitment to peace, while Kyiv's European allies have warned the White House that recent moves could play into Moscow's hands. This month Mr Trump announced a deal to encourage Russian diesel exports and waived sanctions that were due under a law he signed. Hours after Russia signalled readiness to talk, missiles struck Kyiv for the third time in two days. For now, the gap between the two sides remains wide.

That brings us to the technology of this war. Ukraine says speed restrictions on satellite terminals operated by Elon Musk's SpaceX are still in place, despite President Trump saying this week that the limits had been reversed. Since October fourteenth, terminals on SpaceX's secure Starshield network have lost connectivity while travelling above roughly one hundred miles an hour. That disables the fast drones Ukraine uses to strike airfields and fuel depots deep behind Russian lines.

Ukraine's deputy ambassador to the United States, Denys Sienik, said on Friday that nothing has changed, and that every day that passes matters. Trump first said SpaceX had put connectivity back. A day later he said he had not spoken with Mr Musk about it. SpaceX has not publicly explained the restrictions. Ukrainian drone units are adapting mid-campaign, some turning to alternative links.

Now to France. One person was killed and five were wounded in a shooting at a petrol station, a medical centre and a children's playground in Trappes, a suburb of about forty-five thousand people south-west of Paris. The attack lasted about fifteen minutes and also struck the neighbouring commune of La Verriere. The dead man was a young Black man. Among the wounded was a twelve-year-old girl, shot eight times. Three of the injured are in critical condition.

Police arrested a thirty-nine-year-old man with previous convictions. He was known to police, but not to the intelligence services, and did not appear on the national security threat list. A relative was also detained. Police say the weapon was a semi-automatic rifle of an older model, possibly dating from the Second World War. President Emmanuel Macron called the attack racist and terrorist, and described it as a turning point for France. The national counter-terrorism prosecutor has opened an investigation into murder and attempted murder linked to a terrorist enterprise. The mayor of Trappes said the gunman fired at random and wanted to cause a massacre. Trappes is a social-housing suburb where roughly two in five residents are immigrants and unemployment runs near thirty per cent. France remains on high alert for attacks after a violent summer.

In Germany, a major fire broke out on Friday evening at the mountain lodge above Berchtesgaden long known as Hitler's Eagle's Nest. The building, officially called the Kehlsteinhaus, was described as totally alight. Around two hundred firefighters worked the blaze nearly two thousand metres above sea level, in snow and difficult terrain. There were no reports of injuries, and Bavarian authorities do not expect the building to be destroyed. Police detained a suspect on suspicion of arson.

The lodge was built in the latenineteen-thirties and given to Adolf Hitler by the Nazi party as a birthday gift. It was used as a secondary seat of government, where Hitler met commanders to plan the invasion of the Soviet Union. The building survived the war and has operated since nineteen fifty-two as a restaurant and mountain inn, with an exhibition documenting its history as a warning reminder of the crimes of the Nazi regime. It sits on a rocky summit reached by a tunnel and a lift, and draws tourists with views across the Bavarian Alps. Police say the lodge was closed when the fire began.

Back to East Asia, the aftermath of Typhoon Chopai. The storm swept past Taiwan's north-east coast in the early hours of Saturday, killing one man, swept away by waves off the south coast, and injuring five hundred and twenty-seven people. More than one hundred sixty-seven thousand homes lost electricity, though most have been reconnected. Taipei observed a second day of office and school closures, and some flights were still being cancelled.

Once one of the strongest storms of the year in the Pacific, Chopai still carried winds near its centre of about one hundred twenty-five kilometres an hour as it passed. It weakened as it turned north-west toward the Chinese coast, where more disruption is expected, with Japan also in its path. Thousands had been evacuated before it arrived, and hundreds of flights were cancelled across East Asia. Taiwan is generally well prepared for typhoons, which keeps casualties low, even when storms arrive late in the season.

On Wall Street, talk of a merger between SpaceX and Tesla overshadowed both companies' earnings this week. Mr Musk said special committees have been formed at each company to study a combination, and that he is in favour, subject to shareholder approval. He noted the hurdle: half of all Tesla shareholders, not just those who vote, would have to back a deal.

The markets are sceptical. Tesla shares fell three per cent on Thursday and are down seventeen per cent this year. SpaceX fell nine per cent, back below the price set at its market debut in June. SpaceX is valued close to two trillion dollars; Tesla around one and a half trillion, which would make a combination the largest merger on record. Analysts at the firm William Blair wrote that they struggled to find any opportunity that could only be achieved by an official combination. Supporters point to shared batteries, satellite links and a joint chip factory. Legal experts expect steep regulatory hurdles, including antitrust scrutiny, national-security reviews and approvals in China. The merger talk also comes with Mr Musk having committed roughly three hundred sixty-six million dollars to this year's midterm races, while SpaceX holds nearly seven billion dollars in federal contracts this year.

In pharmaceuticals, Moderna shares fell as much as twenty-five per cent after partial results of its closely watched cancer vaccine leaked before the official presentation. The results, in fact, beat Wall Street expectations, and shares of partner Merck also fell. The episode is a reminder of how heavily markets now trade on trial data, and how a leak can move them before doctors and scientists have weighed the evidence in full. The personalised vaccine, called intismeran autogene, is developed with Merck and paired with Merck's immunotherapy Keytruda. In certain melanoma patients, the combination cut the risk of the cancer returning or of death by forty-two per cent, compared with Keytruda alone.

Researchers call it the first late-stage trial showing that a personalised messenger RNA treatment, built around the genetic fingerprint of a patient's own tumour, can prevent recurrence. After two years, eighty-two per cent of patients who received the vaccine alongside the immunotherapy were alive without the melanoma returning, compared with seventy per cent who received the immunotherapy alone. Doctors also note milder side effects than many existing treatments. Analysts said the sell-off reflected inflated expectations rather than a problem with the data; Moderna shares had already risen more than five-fold this year on hopes for the vaccine. It is too early to say whether the treatment helps patients live longer overall. Full results were due at the European oncology conference this weekend.

In corporate news, American Express will cut about five per cent of its global workforce, roughly three thousand eight hundred of its seventy-seven thousand employees, and take a three hundred thirty-six million dollar restructuring charge. The company's finance chief said the cuts would remove management layers and prepare the firm for the artificial intelligence era. Notably, the announcement came alongside a raised profit outlook on strong card spending. The cuts follow a similar move by Barclays, which said a day earlier that it would shed about five thousand jobs, also roughly five per cent of its workforce. Business Insider counts more than thirty-five large companies that have announced layoffs this year, including Meta, Amazon and Walmart. Many have attributed the cuts to efficiency drives and artificial intelligence tools, rather than declining revenue. American Express says the savings should begin to appear next year.

In technology, reviewers are divided over Apple's first folding phone. The Verge scored the nineteen hundred and ninety-nine dollar iPhone Duo seven out of ten, sparking a debate on its own podcast about whether Apple's late entrant justifies its price. CNET called the fold genuinely practical, and the most polished folding phone Apple could have delivered on the first attempt. The device pairs a five-and-a-half inch cover screen with a seven-and-a-half inch inner display, uses a matte inner screen that makes the crease less visible, and adds camera features that use artificial intelligence to pick the best group shot. Comparisons place it in the middle of the current field of folding phones from Samsung and Google. Early supply is limited, and some buyers may wait until January.

A word on the state of American elections, where the courts are moving fast. The Justice Department sued Alaska's election officials on Thursday, seeking to block certification of the state's Senate race unless Republican Senator Dan Sullivan prevails. The suit claims a lookalike candidacy confused voters against Mr Sullivan. A retired teacher also ran under the name Dan Sullivan, and the department alleges that candidacy was meant to draw votes away from the senator and help the Democratic nominee, former representative Mary Peltola. The race could decide control of the Senate. The suit would only take effect if the incumbent loses, and it does not allege fraud. President Trump said Friday that he directed the department to bring the case. Election-law experts called the suit absurdly thin and unlikely to succeed.

Separately, the Supreme Court ruled that student identification cards cannot be used in New Hampshire's midterm election, reviving an April state law after a federal judge had blocked it. The court said the judge should not have changed the rules so close to the November third vote. Justice Ketanji Brown Jackson dissented. New Hampshire's Senate race, between former Republican senator John Sununu and Democratic representative Chris Pappas, could also prove decisive for control of the chamber.

And the Justice Department charged two former Federal Bureau of Investigation officials, Nikki Floris and Bradley Benavides, with making false statements to Congress about the bureau's handling of the Hunter Biden laptop investigation. Both led the bureau's now-disbanded Foreign Influence Task Force, which examined foreign influence operations in the twenty twenty election. Prosecutors filed a criminal complaint rather than a grand jury indictment, and the pair appeared in federal court in Washington on Friday. Their lawyers called the prosecution transparently political, pointing to the timing, less than two weeks before the election. The charges grow out of current FBI director Kash Patel's retrospective review of how the bureau handled the laptop inquiry, claims of a disinformation plot that were later discredited.

Finally, sport. The Milwaukee Brewers beat the Tampa Bay Rays three to two in Game One of the World Series, and only after a video review overturned an apparent game-ending double play in the ninth inning. Replays showed the Brewers' shortstop a fraction off the base, nullifying the double play; pitcher Trevor Megill then struck out the tying run to seal Milwaukee's first World Series victory in forty-four years, and the franchise's first World Series game since nineteen eighty-two. Christian Yelich's run-scoring triple powered the offence, and rookie starter Jacob Misiorowski gave five strong innings. The series pitches two clubs chasing a first championship. Game Two is Saturday night in Milwaukee, with the series then moving to Tampa Bay.
